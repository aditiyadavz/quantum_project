# Section 3 reviews (draft)

Review numbers are provisional. Final IEEE numbers follow first citation in the report.

## Foundations

**[1] Cerezo et al. (2022), "Challenges and opportunities in quantum machine learning."** Problem and objective: This review asks where quantum machine learning (QML) could realistically help data analysis, with emphasis on quantum data from materials science, biochemistry and high-energy physics. Research gap: Early enthusiasm paid less attention to whether these models can actually be trained and how they truly differ from classical learning. Methodology: The authors survey current methods and applications, giving most attention to quantum neural and deep learning models, and contrast them with classical machine learning. Results: As a review it reports no new experiments. Its main message is that trainability remains an open obstacle, while quantum data is named as an especially promising setting. Limitations / scope: The focus on quantum data leaves classical tabular problems, such as medical diagnosis, less directly addressed, so its conclusions need testing on those datasets.

**[2] Schuld and Killoran (2022), "Is Quantum Advantage the Right Goal for Quantum Machine Learning?."** Problem and objective: This perspective questions whether proving quantum advantage should be the main goal of QML research. Research gap: Classical machine learning works well in practice but is hard to analyse, whereas quantum computing cannot yet be tested at realistic scale. Judging practical quantum power is therefore unusually difficult, yet the literature often assumes a race against classical models. Methodology: The authors reason through why the available tools struggle to answer this question, and they point to existing work that takes other viewpoints. Results: They call for a critical debate on the narrative of beating classical models and argue that other research perspectives are an important alternative. No numerical results are presented. Limitations / scope: Being an opinion-based perspective, it gives direction rather than evidence. Its advice still has to be applied through careful, honest studies on real data.

**[3] Huang et al. (2021), "Power of data in quantum machine learning."** Problem and objective: This paper asks when a quantum model can really outperform classical learners once training data is available. Research gap: Most complexity arguments consider computation without data, yet data can make a classically hard function easy to predict. Methodology: The authors derive prediction-error bounds and use them as a test for potential advantage. They also propose a projected quantum kernel model and run numerical tests with up to 30 qubits. Results: With data, classical models were often competitive even on problems tailored to quantum models. The projected model gave a rigorous speed-up in the fault-tolerant setting and a clear near-term advantage on engineered datasets. Limitations / scope: The near-term advantage was shown on datasets deliberately built to favour quantum models, so it does not automatically carry over to real-world data such as clinical records.

**[4] Liu et al. (2021), "A rigorous and robust quantum speed-up in supervised machine learning."** Problem and objective: This work seeks a provable quantum speed-up for supervised classification that needs only classical access to the data. Research gap: Earlier algorithms either assumed quantum access to data or were heuristics with no proof of advantage. Methodology: A conventional support vector machine (SVM) is combined with a fault-tolerant quantum computer that estimates the kernel as a circuit transition amplitude. The authors construct a family of datasets linked to the discrete logarithm problem. Results: Assuming that problem is hard, no classical learner beats random guessing by more than an inverse polynomial, while the quantum classifier reaches high accuracy and tolerates additive kernel errors from finite sampling. Limitations / scope: The proof depends on fault-tolerant hardware and artificially constructed data. It therefore says little about near-term devices or about natural datasets.

**[5] Caro et al. (2022), "Generalization in quantum machine learning from few training data."** Problem and objective: This study examines how well variational QML models generalise after training on only a limited number of examples. Research gap: It was not clearly known how much training data such models need, a key point for applications where data is scarce. Methodology: The authors prove generalisation bounds in terms of the number of trainable gates T, the number of substantially changed gates K, and the number of training points N. Results: The error grows at worst as the square root of T/N and improves to the square root of K/N when few gates change greatly. Unitary compiling and classifying quantum phases with a quantum convolutional network need little data. Limitations / scope: A small generalisation gap does not ensure a low training error or a useful model, and the highlighted applications involve quantum rather than classical data.

**[6] Bowles et al. (2024), "Better than classical? The subtle art of benchmarking quantum machine learning models."** Problem and objective: This preprint tests how far published claims about QML models hold when they are compared systematically with classical baselines. Research gap: Experimental design choices, small scales and commercial narratives make simulated benchmarks hard to interpret reliably. Methodology: The authors built an open-source PennyLane package and tested 12 popular models on 6 binary classification tasks, forming 160 datasets. Results: Out-of-the-box classical models generally beat the quantum classifiers. Removing entanglement often gave equal or better performance, which hints that quantum effects may not be what matters most at these small scales. Five questions for model design are also identified. Limitations / scope: The findings apply to the small tasks that can be simulated today and cannot rule out benefits at larger scale. It was also consulted as a preprint.

## Kernels

**[7] Havlíček et al. (2019), "Supervised learning with quantum-enhanced feature spaces."** Problem and objective: This foundational paper explores using a quantum state space as the feature space for supervised classification. Research gap: Kernel methods become limited when feature spaces grow large and kernels become expensive to compute classically. Methodology: Two approaches were proposed and run on a superconducting processor: a quantum variational classifier, and a quantum kernel estimator whose output trains a classical SVM. Results: Both algorithms were demonstrated experimentally and solved the construction of a classifier. The work presents feature spaces reachable efficiently only on a quantum computer as a possible route to advantage, and it supplies tools for near-term devices. Limitations / scope: The advantage is described only as a possible path, not a proven gain. Real-world performance was left open, which motivates tests on data such as breast cancer records.

**[8] Thanasilp et al. (2024), "Exponential concentration in quantum kernel methods."** Problem and objective: This paper studies the measurement resources needed to estimate quantum kernel values accurately enough for learning. Research gap: The convex training of kernel models is attractive, but it assumes that kernel values can be obtained efficiently from hardware. Methodology: The authors derive analytical concentration bounds for four sources and check them with numerical simulations on several QML tasks. Results: Kernel values for different inputs can concentrate exponentially around a fixed value as qubits increase. With polynomially many measurements, predictions then become independent of the input. Embedding expressivity, global measurements, entanglement and noise are all causes, and kernel alignment can suffer too. Limitations / scope: The bounds hold under certain conditions, and they warn about what to avoid rather than offering a complete design recipe for useful kernels.

**[9] Jerbi et al. (2023), "Quantum machine learning beyond kernel methods."** Problem and objective: This paper seeks a common framework for comparing QML models built from parametrised circuits. Research gap: Understanding of how these model families relate to each other and to classical models was still limited. Methodology: Using quantum information tools, the authors show that all standard models, including data re-uploading circuits, can be mapped to linear models in Hilbert space. They then analyse the qubit and data requirements. Results: For certain learning tasks, linear models need exponentially more qubits than re-uploading models, and kernel methods additionally need exponentially more data points. This separates the models by their resource demands under near-term constraints. Limitations / scope: The separations are proven for particular tasks, so they do not indicate which model will work best on a given practical dataset. Empirical comparison remains necessary.

**[10] Hubregtsen et al. (2022), "Training quantum embedding kernels on near-term quantum computers."** Problem and objective: This work addresses how to choose a suitable quantum kernel and how noise affects it on near-term hardware. Research gap: Kernel methods face three issues: quadratic cost in training samples, difficult kernel selection, and unknown noise effects. The authors tackle the last two. Methodology: They introduce trainable quantum embedding kernels tuned through kernel-target alignment. Device and finite-sampling noise are studied, and mitigation methods are compared numerically and on data from a real processor. Results: Training reduced the training error significantly in the tested setup, and the best mitigation strategy raised the quality of the kernel matrix. Limitations / scope: The quadratic cost of building the kernel matrix was explicitly left unsolved, and the abstract reports its gains for one experimental setup, without numerical values.

**[11] Shaydulin and Wild (2022), "Importance of kernel bandwidth in quantum machine learning."** Problem and objective: This paper investigates how one hyperparameter, kernel bandwidth, shapes the performance of quantum kernels. Research gap: Earlier studies largely overlooked hyperparameters and reported that quantum kernel performance falls as qubits increase. Methodology: The authors identify the parameter that controls bandwidth and test several quantum kernels on classical datasets through large numerical studies, relating the behaviour to classical kernels. Results: Models moved from underfitting at large bandwidth to overfitting at small bandwidth, with the best generalisation in between. Optimising bandwidth reduced the exponential decay of kernel values, so performance improved with qubit count and became competitive with leading classical methods. Limitations / scope: The results come from numerical simulation, so finite sampling and device noise on hardware may reduce the benefit. Bandwidth also adds another setting that must be tuned.

**[12] Peters et al. (2021), "Machine learning of high dimensional data on a noisy quantum processor."** Problem and objective: This experiment tests whether a quantum kernel classifier can handle real high-dimensional data on noisy hardware. Research gap: Earlier demonstrations were small and used synthetic datasets, leaving the practical difficulties of scaling on noisy devices largely unaddressed. Methodology: On Google's Sycamore processor, 17 qubits were used to label 67-dimensional cosmology data without compressing it. They designed a circuit that keeps kernel magnitudes from vanishing and applied error mitigation tailored to kernels. Results: Test-set classification accuracy was comparable to noiseless simulation, showing that careful circuit design and mitigation can preserve useful kernels on real hardware. Limitations / scope: The demonstration concerns a single cosmology dataset and depends on a tailored circuit. Accuracy comparable to simulation does not by itself show an advantage over classical methods.

**[13] Wang et al. (2021), "Towards understanding the power of quantum kernels in the NISQ era."** Problem and objective: This paper asks whether the advantage of quantum kernels survives the noise and limited measurements of near-term machines. Research gap: Earlier results showing lower generalisation error for quantum kernels were mostly established in an ideal setting that ignored these hardware issues. Methodology: The authors analyse kernels theoretically while including system noise and sampling error, propose a remedy based on indefinite kernel learning, and check their results with numerical simulations. Results: The advantage vanishes for large datasets, few measurements and large system noise. The indefinite kernel learning method is offered as a way of preserving the benefit, and the simulations agree with the theory. Limitations / scope: The evidence is theoretical and simulated rather than taken from hardware, and the remedy has not been shown here on practical datasets such as medical records.

## VQA

**[14] Cerezo et al. (2021), "Variational quantum algorithms."** Problem and objective: This review covers variational quantum algorithms (VQAs), which train a parametrised circuit with a classical optimiser. Research gap: Fault-tolerant machines are unlikely to arrive soon, and current devices are limited in qubit number and in circuit depth because of noise. A broad account of how to work within these limits was needed. Methodology: The authors give an overview of the field, its applications and the strategies proposed for overcoming its main difficulties. Results: VQAs have been proposed for nearly every envisioned quantum application and are presented as the leading near-term strategy. Trainability, accuracy and efficiency remain the main challenges. Limitations / scope: As a review it offers no new empirical test, and its optimism about advantage is a prospect that has still to be demonstrated in practical applications.

**[15] Bharti et al. (2022), "Noisy intermediate-scale quantum algorithms."** Problem and objective: This review summarises what can be attempted on noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) computers before error correction becomes available. Research gap: Fully fault-tolerant machines need millions of good qubits and may take decades, whereas noisy devices with limited coherence already exist. A thorough account of their algorithms was therefore useful. Methodology: The authors summarise NISQ paradigms and algorithms across physics, machine learning, chemistry and combinatorial optimisation, along with benchmarking and software tools. Results: The review explains the shared structure of these algorithms along with their limitations and advantages. It gives readers a common map of the field rather than a single new performance result. Limitations / scope: Because the review covers many fields, machine learning receives only part of its attention, and individual claims still require independent benchmarking on specific problems.

**[16] Schuld et al. (2021), "Effect of data encoding on the expressive power of variational quantum-machine-learning models."** Problem and objective: This paper studies how the way data is encoded determines what functions a parametrised quantum circuit can represent. Research gap: Although such circuits had been widely applied, many of their basic theoretical properties as function approximators remained unknown. Methodology: The authors analyse quantum models mathematically by expressing their outputs through a partial Fourier series over the inputs. Results: The available frequencies are set by the data-encoding gates, and repeating simple encoding gates gives access to a richer spectrum. With a sufficiently rich spectrum, some of these models are universal function approximators. Limitations / scope: Being able to represent a function does not guarantee that a model can be trained or that it generalises well. The analysis also concerns expressive power rather than performance on real datasets.

**[17] Pérez-Salinas et al. (2020), "Data re-uploading for a universal quantum classifier."** Problem and objective: This foundational paper asks how much classification power a very small quantum circuit can provide. Research gap: One qubit seems too limited for classifying complex data, because it holds only a simple superposition and its gates are single rotations. Methodology: The authors load the data repeatedly, alternating re-uploading steps with single-qubit processing units and using a classical subroutine for optimisation. They also extend the design to several qubits and benchmark it on varied examples. Results: A single qubit assisted in this way forms a universal quantum classifier. Multi-dimensional inputs and several output classes can be handled, and adding entanglement across qubits improves efficiency. Limitations / scope: The benchmarks are described as examples of complex data rather than a clinical task, and the abstract does not assess hardware noise. Practical medical value therefore remains open.

**[18] Skolik et al. (2021), "Layerwise learning for quantum neural networks."** Problem and objective: This paper looks for a training strategy that makes parametrised quantum circuits easier to optimise on near-term devices. Research gap: The difficult cost landscapes of these circuits, including barren plateaus, make standard full-circuit training unreliable. Methodology: In layerwise learning the circuit depth is increased gradually during optimisation, and only a subset of parameters is updated in each step. The method was tested on a handwritten digit classification task with sampling noise considered. Results: It reached an 8% lower generalisation error on average than standard schemes for circuits of the same size. The share of runs reaching lower test errors was up to 40% larger. Limitations / scope: The evidence comes from one image task, and the approach reduces the risk of plateaus rather than removing it for all circuits and datasets.

## QNN

**[19] Abbas et al. (2021), "The power of quantum neural networks."** Problem and objective: This paper compares the expressibility and trainability of quantum neural networks (QNNs) with those of classical networks. Research gap: The near-term benefits of QML were unclear, and expressibility and trainability of quantum models both needed further study. Methodology: Using information geometry, the authors define an effective dimension based on the Fisher information, prove a generalisation bound, and connect the Fisher spectrum to barren plateaus. Results were also verified on real quantum hardware. Results: Well-designed QNNs reached a significantly higher effective dimension than comparable classical networks. Certain QNNs also resisted barren plateaus and trained faster, owing to a more evenly spread Fisher spectrum. Limitations / scope: The benefit is stated for well-designed networks, not QNNs in general, and higher effective dimension does not automatically produce better accuracy on practical data.

**[20] Cerezo et al. (2021), "Cost function dependent barren plateaus in shallow parametrized quantum circuits."** Problem and objective: This paper asks how the choice of cost function affects gradient size in shallow variational circuits. Research gap: VQAs are heuristic methods whose scaling was unproven, and it was unclear whether shallow circuits would avoid vanishing gradients. Methodology: The authors prove two results for alternating layered ansatz circuits built from blocks forming local 2-designs, then illustrate them with quantum autoencoder simulations of up to 100 qubits. Results: Global observables produce exponentially vanishing gradients even at shallow depth. Local observables give at worst polynomially vanishing gradients when depth is logarithmic in the number of qubits, which links locality to trainability. Limitations / scope: The proofs hold for a specific class of ansatz, and they address trainability alone. A trainable cost function may still fail to give good accuracy on a real task.

**[21] Wang et al. (2021), "Noise-induced barren plateaus in variational quantum algorithms."** Problem and objective: This paper examines whether hardware noise places a fundamental limit on variational quantum algorithms. Research gap: Earlier barren plateau work linked vanishing gradients to random parameter initialisation in noise-free circuits, leaving the effect of noise itself unsettled. Methodology: The authors give a rigorous proof for local Pauli noise, covering a generic ansatz that includes alternating operator and unitary coupled cluster circuits. Numerical work with a realistic hardware noise model supports it. Results: When circuit depth grows linearly with qubit number, gradients shrink exponentially with qubit count. These noise-induced plateaus differ in concept from the noise-free kind. Limitations / scope: The proof assumes a particular noise model and depth scaling. It warns against deep circuits but does not by itself show how much shallow, small circuits are affected.

**[22] Holmes et al. (2022), "Connecting Ansatz Expressibility to Gradient Magnitudes and Barren Plateaus."** Problem and objective: This paper explores the relationship between how expressive an ansatz is and how large its gradients are. Research gap: Barren plateau results were known for ansatze forming exact 2-designs, but not for arbitrary circuits used in practice. Methodology: The authors extend the analysis to general ansatze, calculating gradient variance in terms of the distance of the ansatz from a 2-design, and they provide numerical illustrations. Results: The bounds indicate that highly expressive ansatze have flatter cost landscapes and are harder to train. Expressibility therefore comes at a cost, and this must be considered when designing circuits that avoid plateaus. Limitations / scope: The analysis concerns gradient behaviour and does not identify the best ansatz for a particular dataset. Less expressive circuits may also be unable to represent the required solution.

## Hybrid

**[23] Mari et al. (2020), "Transfer learning in hybrid classical-quantum neural networks."** Problem and objective: This foundational paper applies transfer learning to networks that combine classical and quantum parts. Research gap: Intermediate-scale quantum devices cannot process high-dimensional data such as images directly. Methodology: Several hybrid schemes are proposed, mainly a pre-trained classical network followed by a final variational quantum circuit. Proof-of-concept examples cover image recognition and quantum state classification, with PennyLane experiments on IBM and Rigetti computers. Results: The classical part can pre-process large inputs and pass a small set of informative features to the quantum processor, and a high-resolution image classifier was tested on two real quantum computers. Limitations / scope: These are proofs of concept, and the classical network carries much of the feature extraction, so the specific contribution of the quantum layer needs separate evaluation.

**[24] Liu et al. (2021), "Hybrid quantum-classical convolutional neural networks."** Problem and objective: This paper designs a quantum-classical analogue of a convolutional neural network that suits current noisy devices. Research gap: Deep learning recognises patterns well, and quantum circuits can produce distributions that are hard to generate classically, yet the two needed a practical bridge that respects limited qubits and depth. Methodology: The authors propose a hybrid network that uses quantum circuits to enhance feature mapping, with a framework that computes gradients of hybrid loss functions automatically. It was tested on a Tetris dataset. Results: The hybrid network exceeded the learning accuracy of a classical CNN with the same structure while keeping nonlinearity and scalability. Limitations / scope: The Tetris dataset is simple and the classical comparison used the same structure only, so a stronger tuned baseline might narrow the reported gap.

**[25] Hur et al. (2022), "Quantum convolutional neural network for classical data classification."** Problem and objective: This study benchmarks fully parameterised quantum convolutional neural networks on classical data. Research gap: Many quantum analogues of the CNN had been proposed, but how design choices affect classical data classification needed systematic comparison. Methodology: Using only two-qubit interactions, the authors varied circuit structures, data encoding, classical pre-processing, cost functions and optimisers on MNIST and Fashion-MNIST. Results: Accuracy was excellent in most cases despite few free parameters, and the quantum networks performed noticeably better than CNNs under similar training conditions. Their shallow circuits suit near-term devices. Limitations / scope: The result compares models trained under similar conditions, which may favour small models, and the datasets are image benchmarks. Results may differ for tabular medical features or under real device noise.

**[26] Jäger and Krems (2023), "Universal expressiveness of variational quantum classifiers and quantum kernels for support vector machines."** Problem and objective: This paper asks how far variational classifiers and quantum kernel SVMs can, in principle, extend quantum advantage. Research gap: The search for advantage in QML models needed a precise theoretical statement of what these model types can solve. Methodology: The authors construct a classification problem from the k-Forrelation problem, which is PromiseBQP-complete, and show that both model types can solve it. Results: A suitable feature map and quantum kernel exist that make these models efficient solvers for any BQP problem. They can therefore be designed for an advantage on classification problems that are quantum-tractable but classically intractable. Limitations / scope: This is an existence result about designed feature maps. It gives no route to finding one for natural data, and it reports no performance on practical datasets.

**[27] Ren et al. (2022), "Experimental quantum adversarial learning with programmable superconducting qubits."** Problem and objective: This experiment tests whether quantum classifiers are vulnerable to small, deliberately crafted changes in their input. Research gap: Theory predicted this weakness, which matters in safety-critical settings, but it had not been demonstrated experimentally. Methodology: The authors trained variational classifiers on ten transmon qubits, with average two-qubit gate fidelity above 99.4%, using real images including medical MRI scans as well as quantum data. Results: Classifiers reaching test accuracy up to 99% were deceived by small adversarial perturbations, and adversarial training substantially improved their robustness. Limitations / scope: The work addresses security, not whether quantum classifiers outperform classical ones. Protection was shown against the perturbations studied, and wider clinical robustness remains open.

## Health

**[28] Moradi et al. (2022), "Clinical data classification with noisy intermediate scale quantum computers."** Problem and objective: This study assesses whether QML is feasible on real clinical datasets using actual hardware. Research gap: Progress in QML had not been matched by broad testing on open clinical data with near-term devices. Methodology: The authors propose a quantum distance classifier and a simplified quantum-kernel SVM. Data was embedded with logarithmic encoding and run on the 15-qubit IBMQ Melbourne computer, then compared with earlier QML and classical methods on three open clinical datasets. Results: The distance classifier did better on datasets with few samples and features, and kernel methods did better on larger ones. The encoding raised the area under the ROC curve by up to 2%. Limitations / scope: The abstract reports relative comparisons rather than a clear win over classical models, and the 2% gain is small and specific to the tested datasets.

**[29] Azevedo et al. (2022), "Quantum transfer learning for breast cancer detection."** Problem and objective: This work classifies full-image mammograms from the BCDR collection as malignant or benign with a hybrid quantum model. Research gap: The authors wanted to learn whether the transfer learning idea of Mari et al. could benefit a hard medical imaging task. Methodology: They trained a set of hybrid classical-quantum networks with transfer learning, used heatmaps to inspect the image regions targeted, evaluated several metrics, and also ran experiments on a real quantum device. Results: Accuracy reached 84%, against 76.9% for the state-of-the-art work they compared with. Some architectures suited particular tasks better, and the method may help generalisation on complex data. Limitations / scope: The authors themselves state that further tests are needed. Much of the feature extraction is classical, so the gain cannot be credited to the quantum part alone.

**[30] P et al. (2024), "Enhanced QSVM with elitist non‐dominated sorting genetic optimisation algorithm for breast cancer diagnosis."** Problem and objective: This paper applies a quantum SVM to the binary diagnosis of malignant breast cancer. Research gap: The authors aim to improve on using a quantum SVM alone by also optimising its parameters against several objectives. Methodology: The quantum SVM is combined with an elitist non-dominated sorting genetic algorithm that tunes its parameters, and quantum kernel estimation is run in an IBM QSVM simulator. Results: The authors report remarkably accurate results compared with a classical SVM and an execution time of 0.14, although the abstract states neither a specific accuracy figure nor a unit for that time. Limitations / scope: Without those figures in the abstract, the size of the claimed gain cannot be judged from it. Execution on a simulator also leaves real hardware noise unexamined.

**[31] Akpinar et al. (2024), "Evaluating the Impact of Different Quantum Kernels on the Classification Performance of Support Vector Machine Algorithm: A Medical Dataset Application."** Problem and objective: This preprint measures how the choice of quantum feature map changes the classification results of a quantum kernel SVM on medical data. Research gap: Encoding classical data is essential, but its specific effect on classification outcomes had been little explored. Methodology: The authors computed kernel matrices from nine feature maps and applied them to the original Wisconsin Breast Cancer dataset and the TCGA Glioma dataset, comparing classifier performance and execution time. Results: Feature maps built on Rx gates worked best for the Wisconsin data, and Ry gates worked best for Glioma, on both performance and time. The choice of map clearly affected outcomes. Limitations / scope: It uses the original Wisconsin dataset rather than the Diagnostic version studied here, compares no wider classical model set in its abstract, and is a preprint.

**[32] Zorlu and Colak (2026), "A Robustness-Oriented Quantum–Classical Hybrid Machine Learning Pipeline for Breast Cancer Diagnosis: External Validation, Explainability, and Rigorous Benchmarking in the NISQ Era."** Problem and objective: This study evaluates quantum models for breast cancer diagnosis under a deliberately strict protocol, without expecting them to win. Research gap: Many comparisons use one internal hold-out result and omit calibration, decision value and explainability. Methodology: Using WDBC for development and an independent Wisconsin cohort for external validation, it compared five classical learners, an eight-qubit VQC, a ZZ-feature-map QSVM and a hybrid ensemble. Results: Internally the RBF SVM reached AUC 0.998, while the QSVM reached only 0.727. Externally the VQC scored 0.983, with intervals overlapping the best models. Z-rotation embeddings collapsed the VQC to chance. Limitations / scope: The authors stress that curated benchmark data does not establish clinical readiness, and the classical models are already close to the performance ceiling on this dataset.

**[33] Krunic et al. (2022), "Quantum Kernels for Real-World Predictions Based on Electronic Health Records."** Problem and objective: This work investigates whether empirical quantum advantage can be found on real healthcare data. Research gap: Provable advantages existed for synthetic data, but it had not been studied empirically whether advantage is attainable, or on which data. Methodology: The authors built an end-to-end framework over electronic health record subsets with 5 to 20 features and 200 to 300 training samples. RBF SVMs were compared with quantum kernel models run on an IBM quantum computer. Results: Regimes where quantum kernels might help were identified, and a new terrain ruggedness index was proposed for estimating how a model will perform on a dataset. Limitations / scope: The framework identifies where advantage could occur for selected subsets, not across clinical data generally. Further validation on independent cohorts is therefore still required.

**[34] Landman et al. (2022), "Quantum Methods for Neural Networks and Application to Medical Image Classification."** Problem and objective: This paper introduces new quantum neural network methods and tests them on medical image classification. Research gap: Quantum methods needed efficient training procedures and direct comparison with classical networks on real hardware. Methodology: The authors present an orthogonal network based on a pyramidal circuit, and quantum-assisted networks that use a quantum computer for inner product estimation. They compared quantum and classical methods on hardware and simulators. Results: Quantum and classical neural networks gave a similar level of accuracy, and the new training algorithms were proven to scale asymptotically better than earlier ones. Limitations / scope: Similar accuracy is not an advantage, and the authors tie the promise of these methods to the arrival of better quantum hardware. Practical benefit in imaging thus remains prospective.

## Image

**[35] Henderson et al. (2020), "Quanvolutional neural networks: powering image recognition with quantum circuits."** Problem and objective: This foundational paper introduces a quantum layer for extracting image features within a convolutional architecture. Research gap: CNNs depend on hierarchical feature extraction, and it was unknown whether small quantum transformations could usefully contribute to this on near-term devices. Methodology: Quanvolutional layers transform local patches of input with random quantum circuits. Three model types were compared on MNIST: CNNs, quanvolutional networks, and CNNs with added non-linearities. Results: The quanvolutional models gave higher test accuracy and faster training than the purely classical CNNs. The small circuits need little or no error correction. Limitations / scope: MNIST is an easy benchmark and the abstract's accuracy claim is limited to those models. Whether the benefit is uniquely quantum, and whether it survives on harder images, is not settled.

**[36] Senokosov et al. (2024), "Quantum machine learning for image classification."** Problem and objective: This study develops two hybrid quantum models for image classification that can operate within near-term limits. Research gap: Circuits with many qubits are not yet feasible, so image classification needs designs built from smaller quantum parts. Methodology: The first model uses parallel quantum circuits and was tested on MNIST, Medical MNIST and CIFAR-10. The second uses a quanvolutional layer to reduce image resolution. Results: The first model reached 99.21% on full MNIST using one-eighth of the parameters of the matching classical model, above 99% on Medical MNIST and above 82% on CIFAR-10. The second equalled the classical version with a quarter of the parameters. Limitations / scope: The models are heavily hybrid, so results cannot be attributed to the quantum layers alone, and their performance on noisy hardware is not reported in the abstract.

**[37] Wei et al. (2022), "A quantum convolutional neural network on NISQ devices."** Problem and objective: This paper proposes a quantum convolutional network that lowers computational complexity on NISQ devices. Research gap: Near-term models must be practical in size and noise tolerance, and they need implementable circuits that correspond to specific classical kernels. Methodology: The design uses a number of basic gates polylogarithmic in input size. Numerical simulations cover spatial filtering, namely smoothing, sharpening and edge detection, as well as handwritten number recognition. Results: Complexity is greatly reduced compared with classical counterparts, the model tolerates certain noise in image recognition, and its parameters do not depend on input size. It maps classical filters directly to quantum circuits. Limitations / scope: Results come from numerical simulation on simple image tasks, the noise robustness applies only to certain noise, and costs of loading data are not discussed in the abstract.

## Optimization

**[38] Blekos et al. (2024), "A review on Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm and its variants."** Problem and objective: This review assesses the Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA) and its variants for combinatorial optimisation. Research gap: Researchers needed clear guidance on whether QAOA can beat classical algorithms and in which circumstances it should be used. Methodology: The authors overview QAOA performance across problem types and scenarios, examine hardware concerns, including how errors and noise affect it, and carry out a comparative study of selected extensions. Results: The review presents the current state of the algorithm and its variants, along with future directions and a short practical guide for applying it. Limitations / scope: The abstract treats classical competitiveness as a question to examine rather than a settled result. The review format also means that claims depend on the varied studies that it collects.

**[39] Ebadi et al. (2022), "Quantum optimization of maximum independent set using Rydberg atom arrays."** Problem and objective: This experiment searches for a quantum speed-up on a hard practical problem, maximum independent set. Research gap: Realising a speed-up on relevant, computationally hard problems remains a central open challenge. Methodology: Two-dimensional arrays of Rydberg atoms, reaching 289 qubits, were programmed with an encoding based on the blockade effect, and variational algorithms were optimised in a closed loop on graphs of chosen connectivity. Results were benchmarked against simulated annealing. Results: Problem hardness was governed by solution degeneracy and the number of local minima. On the hardest graphs, the team saw a superlinear speed-up for exact solutions when circuits were deep. Limitations / scope: The comparison is with simulated annealing specifically, on one class of graphs, so it does not establish an advantage over every classical solver.

**[40] Abbas et al. (2024), "Challenges and opportunities in quantum optimization."** Problem and objective: This perspective reviews where quantum algorithms may help optimisation and how such claims should be tested. Research gap: Interest in quantum optimisation is widespread, but approaches differ across fields and comparisons with classical methods need clear standards. Methodology: The authors use complexity theory to separate provably exact from heuristic settings, outline algorithmic building blocks and problem classes, and analyse the effects of scaling on noisy devices. Results: They identify key open questions, propose metrics for fair classical comparison, and suggest finance and sustainability as sources of benchmark problems. Limitations / scope: It sets out an agenda rather than demonstrating a new advantage, and its metrics and benchmarks must still be adopted and tested by future experimental work.

## AIforQC

**[41] Bausch et al. (2024), "Learning high-accuracy error decoding for quantum processors."** Problem and objective: This paper applies machine learning to decoding errors in the surface code, a leading scheme for quantum error correction. Research gap: Decoding noisy syndrome information accurately is a key obstacle to large-scale fault-tolerant machines, and human-designed decoders may not capture complex real errors. Methodology: The authors developed a recurrent, transformer-based neural decoder. It was trained first on approximate synthetic data and then refined on a small number of experimental samples. Results: It outperformed other leading decoders on real data from Google's Sycamore processor for distances 3 and 5, and kept its advantage on simulated data up to distance 11 with realistic cross-talk and leakage. Limitations / scope: Beyond distance 5 the evidence is from simulation, and the abstract does not settle whether decoding is fast enough at larger scale for practical machines.

**[42] Alexeev et al. (2025), "Artificial intelligence for quantum computing."** Problem and objective: This review examines how artificial intelligence can help build and operate useful quantum computers. Research gap: Applying leading AI methods to quantum computing requires expertise from two demanding fields that rarely meet, so a shared account was needed. Methodology: The authors review state-of-the-art AI techniques across the hardware and software stack, from device design through to applications, then assess future opportunities and obstacles. Results: The review argues that many of the largest scaling challenges in quantum computing may depend on progress in AI, and it shows that AI methods are already advancing several parts of the stack. Limitations / scope: As a review it does not provide a new quantitative test, and it addresses AI serving quantum computing rather than quantum models improving classical prediction tasks.

## Applications

**[43] Herman et al. (2023), "Quantum computing for finance."** Problem and objective: This review summarises the state of quantum computing for financial applications, written for physicists. Research gap: Physicists need an account of the classical techniques used in finance and of where quantum methods could genuinely help. Methodology: The authors cover quantum algorithms for stochastic modelling, optimisation and machine learning, and they discuss potential advantages, limitations and open challenges. Results: Monte Carlo integration and gradient estimation on quantum machines may run quadratically faster than classical versions, while financial optimisation can take continuous, discrete or mixed forms. The benefits and difficulties of QML for classical problems also apply in finance. Limitations / scope: The quadratic speed-up still requires reduced quantum resources before it could become feasible even on early fault-tolerant machines, so practical gains in finance remain in the future.

**[44] Harwood et al. (2021), "Formulating and Solving Routing Problems on Quantum Computers."** Problem and objective: This paper studies how logistics routing problems should be formulated for quantum computers. Research gap: Vehicle routing is NP-hard and well served by classical methods, while the suitability of quantum algorithms and formulations required exploration. Methodology: The authors propose several formulations that treat inventory routing as vehicle routing under time-window constraints. They compare the resulting quadratic unconstrained binary problems using difficulty metrics, then solve them on simulated devices with quantum algorithms including VQE and QAOA. Results: Each formulation has strengths and weaknesses, and the simulated runs showed the relative benefits and robustness of the different algorithms. Limitations / scope: The solutions come from simulated rather than physical devices, and the abstract does not claim superiority over classical routing methods, so practical logistics value remains unproven.

**[45] Kalinin and Krundyshev (2023), "Security intrusion detection using quantum machine learning techniques."** Problem and objective: This paper evaluates QML for detecting security intrusions when network data becomes very large. Research gap: Conventional machine learning on ordinary hardware can become slow or lose accuracy on inputs of a million samples or more. Methodology: Quantum SVM and quantum convolutional networks were compared with conventional detectors, using the authors' own dataset. Their software groups network packets into streams and encodes them for quantum computing. Results: The authors report 98% accuracy and processing twice as fast as the conventional algorithms applied to the same task. Limitations / scope: Results rest on a dataset created by the authors, which makes independent comparison harder, and the abstract does not explain how the speed comparison treats simulation or hardware.
